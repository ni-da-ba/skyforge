# Lower Sea — Worldbuilding Direction

**Status:** accepted worldbuilding direction; intentionally incomplete cosmology  
**Scope:** setting truth and tone, not a Bootstrap implementation requirement

## Core fact

The inhabited sky is not suspended over an abstract void.

Far beneath the ordinary archipelagos and flight routes lies a permanent deep-cloud region, and beneath that lies the **Lower Sea**. A vessel that falls far enough does not fall out of the world; it falls deeper into it.

The Lower Sea is physically real. Its full extent, depth, ecology, history, and ultimate significance are deliberately unknown.

## Vertical world image

```text
upper / inhabited sky
    floating islands
    settlements
    Guild routes
    ordinary aviation

        ↓

lower sky
    increasingly difficult recovery
    worsening visibility and weather

        ↓

permanent deep cloud
    rain
    turbulence
    little or no useful horizon

        ↓

LOWER SEA
    black water
    wind
    rain
    rare black-rock relief

        ↓

seabed / old land below
    largely unknown
```

The exact physical distances and atmospheric layers remain unresolved.

## Tone

The Lower Sea should not be treated as a conventional hostile biome or a place that must immediately be filled with creatures, combat encounters, or spectacle.

Its defining vocabulary is:

> **Wind.**  
> **Rain.**  
> **Black rock.**  
> **Black water.**

Its horror should come primarily from scale, exposure, weather, darkness, and absence.

The water need not be literally black. Under a permanent cloud deck, with little direct light and enormous apparent depth, it should read as black or near-black at distance. The world below should feel materially real and deeply indifferent rather than theatrically evil.

Visible large life should be absent or extremely rare by default. If exceptional phenomena or organisms are ever introduced, rarity and uncertainty should do most of the work. The setting should resist explaining every unusual observation.

## Falling and loss

Falling is not annihilation into a void.

A disabled vessel may descend through ordinary airspace, into low sky, through the cloud deck, and eventually toward the Lower Sea. Recovery can become progressively more difficult with depth.

A vessel that disappears below practical recovery or observation may be recorded as **lost below** rather than automatically confirmed destroyed. Its actual fate may be unknown.

Possible outcomes include:

- recovery before deep-cloud entry;
- controlled or uncontrolled descent into the cloud layer;
- interception by a recovery vessel;
- impact with rare lower relief;
- water impact;
- temporary flotation;
- breakup, sinking, or later recovery.

This gives transponders, recorders, last-known position, weather, and recovery operations genuine cultural and practical importance.

## Sky Dredgers

The **Sky Dredger** is an established concept: a specialized recovery vessel built to retrieve lost aircraft, cargo, wreckage, or survivors from below ordinary flight routes and, in exceptional cases, from the Lower Sea itself.

Sky Dredgers should reinforce the reality of the lower world without making it ordinary. Their crews may know the operational hazards of descent and salvage extremely well while still knowing very little about the sea as a whole.

The profession should feel closer to deep-ocean salvage than monster hunting.

## The Dark Mountains

Rare masses of black rock may rise from the Lower Sea or penetrate the undercloud region. These are provisionally referred to as the **Dark Mountains**.

They are exceptional locations, not routine terrain.

A Dark Mountain may eventually support a late- or endgame expedition such as a geological or survey mission. The significance of such an expedition is that the player finally reaches a place from the lower world that can be physically occupied and studied, not that the mountain must contain a dungeon or boss.

A first visit should preserve the core Lower Sea vocabulary: rain, wind, black rock, black water, and a horizon with very little else on it.

The Dark Mountains may ultimately help answer questions about the relationship between floating-island geology and the old lower world, but that relationship is not fixed by this note.

## The two graveyards

The setting has a deliberate poetic symmetry:

> **The sea is the graveyard of the sky.**  
> **The seabed is the graveyard of the sea.**

Airships and aircraft that fall may ultimately enter the Lower Sea. Ships that founder there descend farther still, toward the land or seabed below.

This gives the vertical world a sense of accumulated history: things are not simply deleted when they fail. They go downward and become part of progressively less accessible layers of the world.

The resulting fear of falling is therefore not merely fear of death. It is the knowledge that there is somewhere beneath civilization to fall *into*.

## Tonal role in Skyforge

The Lower Sea is a serious counterweight to Skyforge's whimsy, engineering play, bright adventure, and earned wonder.

It should deepen those qualities rather than replace them with grimness. The upper world remains a place of discovery, invention, travel, ecology, commerce, and beauty. The lower world provides consequence, scale, age, and uncertainty.

A useful thematic shorthand is:

> **Wonder above; consequence below.**

This is a tendency, not an absolute metaphysical law.

## Future implementation possibility

Direct Lower Sea play is **not** required for Bootstrap or ordinary progression.

A future implementation could represent extreme descent through the deep cloud as a seamless-feeling transition into a custom Lower Sea dimension. Dense cloud, low visibility, weather, and instrument flight could conceal the technical boundary while preserving the fiction of continuous vertical travel.

This is only an implementation possibility, not a current commitment.

Likewise, a future Dark Mountain survey could expose a very small, authored lower-world location long before the project ever attempts a complete Lower Sea dimension.

## Intentionally unresolved

Do **not** casually close these questions merely to add content:

- the exact depth or global extent of the Lower Sea;
- the complete topology of the seabed;
- what, if anything, lives in the deep water;
- how much of the Lower Sea has actually been surveyed;
- whether permanent human settlements exist below the cloud deck;
- the exact origin and age of the Dark Mountains;
- the exact relationship between lower-world geology and floating islands;
- the ultimate cause of island suspension/uplift;
- whether unusual dredger reports are mundane, misunderstood, or genuinely exceptional.

The Lower Sea should remain genuinely underexplained even after players eventually reach it.

## Design constraint

When adding future Lower Sea material, prefer **absence, weather, scale, and physical consequence** over encounter density.

Do not turn the Lower Sea into a generic monster ocean simply because an empty space exists to populate.
