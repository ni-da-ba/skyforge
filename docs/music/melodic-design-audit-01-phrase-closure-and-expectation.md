# MUS-0001 — Melodic design audit 01: phrase closure, contour, and expectation

**Status:** Adopted as a design constraint for the next Track 03 theme pass  
**Scope:** melody and phrase design only; no orchestration changes  
**Reason:** First-Principles Theme 02 improved continuity, but exposed a recurring bias toward ending phrases near or on registral highs when the listener expected melodic settling.

## Problem statement

The failure is not that high phrase endings are intrinsically wrong.

The failure is that recent Track 03 drafts repeatedly used register to communicate continuation:

- phrase climbs;
- phrase reaches or approaches a local high;
- phrase ends there;
- next phrase is expected to explain the unresolved height.

That can work occasionally, but it became a default. The result is a melody that repeatedly sounds as though it has stopped at its own climax.

For this cue, the listener often expects a local high point to be followed by a reversal, descent, or registral return before the phrase boundary. When that expected settling is withheld at multiple phrase endings, the line feels authored against the listener's ear rather than with it.

The next pass must therefore separate three functions that had been conflated:

1. **apex** — where a phrase reaches its local expressive high;
2. **cadential arrival** — where the phrase sounds complete or provisionally complete;
3. **continuation pressure** — what makes the next phrase necessary.

A phrase can descend and still remain open. Continuation pressure should usually come from harmony, scale-degree function, rhythm, or motivic incompletion rather than from ending high by default.

## Research basis

This audit draws on:

- William Caplin's formal-function tradition as represented in Open Music Theory's treatment of periods and sentences;
- classical cadence pedagogy from Open Music Theory, University of Puget Sound, and UCSB;
- Eugene Narmour's implication-realization model;
- E. Glenn Schellenberg's simplified melodic-expectancy model;
- David Huron's work on musical expectation and melodic contour;
- Tierney, Russo, and Patel's empirical work on phrase-final lengthening and melodic arch tendencies;
- public-domain score inspection of Mozart K.331, I and Beethoven Op.13, II;
- the existing Skyforge references: *Rosalina in the Observatory*, *Space Junk Road*, and the *Outer Wilds* score, used as stylistic references rather than as note-for-note templates.

## 1. Cadence is not register

The strongest correction is conceptual:

> An open phrase does not need to end high.

In tonal phrase design, cadence is a combined harmonic, melodic, metrical, and rhythmic event. A half cadence is open because it ends on dominant harmony. Its melody can end on scale degree 2, 7, 5, or even 4 depending on context. None of those requires the singer or instrument to sit at the top of the phrase's tessitura.

Likewise, an authentic cadence becomes especially conclusive when the melody arrives on tonic over tonic harmony. That arrival is often approached stepwise and can occur below an earlier phrase apex.

For Track 03 this means:

- use harmony to distinguish open and closed phrase endings;
- do not use registral height as the primary open/closed switch;
- allow an antecedent-like phrase to descend into a low or middle-register scale degree 2 or 5 over dominant;
- reserve high endings for specific expressive questions, suspended states, or deliberate denial of expected reversal.

## 2. Apex and cadence should normally be separated

The public-domain theme of Mozart K.331 illustrates a core formal principle: the first and second phrases differ in cadential strength, not simply in whether the melody finishes high or low. The thematic surface contains interior ornaments and local peaks, while the phrase boundaries are clarified by cadence.

The opening of Beethoven's *Pathétique* slow movement is similarly useful to inspect visually. Its lyric line repeatedly rises to salient tones and then continues away from them. The high point is part of a phrase's expressive shape, not automatically its final punctuation.

For the contemplative Track 03 role, the working default should therefore be:

> rise toward the emotional point, then let the phrase come down enough to sound spoken rather than interrupted.

This is not a universal law. It is a role-appropriate default for a lyrical, reflective cue.

## 3. Empirical contour evidence supports the listener's complaint

Huron's work on Western folk melody found a strong aggregate tendency toward arch-like phrase shapes: initial rise, a higher interior region, and final fall. Tierney and colleagues likewise discuss arch-shaped and descending contours together with relatively long phrase-final notes.

A recent 2026 contour study cautions against treating a small number of named contour types as natural discrete categories. That warning matters: the next Skyforge pass should **not** turn "arch" into another mechanical recipe.

The useful conclusion is weaker and more practical:

- final descent is common enough to be a strong stylistic prior;
- a phrase ending at its highest point is marked behavior, not a neutral default;
- for a contemplative melody, repeated high endings will predictably feel less settled unless another parameter clearly supplies closure.

## 4. Large leaps create obligations

Narmour's implication-realization theory and Schellenberg's later simplified two-factor model both formalize a listener tendency highly relevant to the recent drafts.

Schellenberg's two especially robust factors are:

- **pitch proximity** — nearby continuations are generally more expected;
- **pitch reversal** — after a large interval, listeners often expect a change of direction.

In ordinary compositional terms:

> a large upward leap tends to ask for a smaller downward continuation.

This is exactly where the high-ending bias becomes costly.

If a phrase leaps upward and then simply stops near the new registral extreme, the ear may still be waiting for the reversal or gap-fill. Ending there can work as intentional suspension, but it should not be our routine way of chaining phrases.

### Track 03 rule

After an upward leap of roughly a fourth or larger:

- normally give the line a stepwise or small-interval reversal before the phrase closes;
- if the high note is intentionally phrase-final, mark that as a deliberate open gesture and do not repeat the device at the next phrase boundary;
- do not place the next phrase start there merely because the previous phrase ran out of measures.

## 5. Predictability belongs at the right scale

Huron's expectation work is useful because it distinguishes musical satisfaction from mere repetition. Listeners can experience pleasure when predictions are fulfilled, while too much obvious predictability becomes banal.

For this cue, the best division of labor is:

- **phrase identity:** predictable enough to recognize;
- **cadential direction:** usually satisfy the listener's low-level expectation;
- **interior detail:** where surprise belongs;
- **harmony underneath a familiar contour:** where reinterpretation belongs;
- **large-scale return:** recognizable but not copied wholesale.

This is the opposite of several weaker Track 03 passes, where the phrase ending was made surprising while the middle of the phrase was comparatively arbitrary.

### New priority

> Make the ending feel earned; put the interesting deviation before it.

## 6. Sentence and period models clarify two different kinds of momentum

The period and sentence are not templates we need to imitate literally, but they expose two useful ways phrases create inevitability.

### Period logic

A period places a less conclusive cadence before a more conclusive one. The first phrase can feel finished enough to be intelligible while still demanding a response.

For Track 03 this means the first phrase may **descend and settle** while remaining harmonically open.

That is preferable to making it end high solely to communicate "question."

### Sentence logic

In a sentence, a basic idea is presented and then continuation gains momentum by fragmentation, liquidation, sequential repetition, faster melodic rhythm, and/or faster harmonic rhythm before cadence.

Open Music Theory's Mozart K.283 example is particularly relevant: the continuation fragments the characteristic material and ultimately uses conventional cadential material with a **descending contour**.

That gives us an important correction:

> forward drive and final descent are compatible.

We do not need a rising phrase ending to preserve momentum.

## 7. Phrase-final timing matters as much as pitch

Empirical work on melodic expectation shows that onset timing contributes strongly to expectation and emotional response, and research on phrase structure repeatedly finds phrase-final lengthening.

For the next Track 03 theme pass, phrase endings should therefore normally receive at least two of these cues:

- longer final or penultimate duration;
- lower onset density;
- arrival on a metrically meaningful position;
- reduction of ornamental activity;
- harmonic arrival or dominant pause;
- downward or registrally returning contour.

A phrase ending should sound like a punctuation mark before the listener analyzes the notes.

## 8. Why First-Principles Theme 02 misfired

The pass correctly tried to create hinges between phrases, but the method overvalued continuity.

The design repeatedly asked the final pitch of one phrase to become the seed of the next. That produced several local consequences:

- phrase endings were selected partly for their usefulness as launch points;
- high or energized pitches were preserved at boundaries because they offered obvious continuation;
- the melody sometimes sacrificed a natural settling gesture to keep the chain visibly connected;
- phrase connection became a graph problem rather than a rhetorical one.

This confuses **continuity** with **inevitability**.

A listener does not need literal shared-pitch linkage at every boundary. A low, satisfying cadence can still make the next phrase inevitable if:

- the harmony remains on V;
- a motive is left incomplete;
- the accompaniment supplies a pickup;
- a low cadence tone becomes an anacrusis source through neighbor motion;
- the next phrase answers a previously established question;
- the larger dynamic or registral arc is still unfinished.

### Corrected rule

> Phrase linkage is semantic and expectational, not necessarily literal.

## 9. Studied-example takeaways

### Mozart, Piano Sonata K.331, I

Formal pedagogy identifies the opening as a period with a less-conclusive first cadence and more-conclusive second cadence. The basic idea is repeated with recognizable rhythmic and contour identity.

**Use for Skyforge:**

- preserve a recognizable phrase opening;
- differentiate endings by cadence/function;
- allow the phrase to settle rather than forcing register to carry formal meaning.

### Mozart, Piano Sonata K.283, I

Open Music Theory treats the opening as a sentence. Characteristic material is fragmented in continuation, melodic/harmonic rhythm accelerate, and the cadential phase ends with a descending contour.

**Use for Skyforge:**

- development can generate forward motion;
- cadence can still descend;
- final descent does not imply loss of momentum.

### Beethoven, Piano Sonata Op.13, II

Inspection of the public-domain score shows an overtly lyrical model in which high notes are expressive interior events within longer slurred spans. The line does not rely on repeated terminal highs to maintain continuity.

**Use for Skyforge:**

- give local apexes room to resolve;
- phrase endings should sound sung/breathed, not clipped at the top.

### Huron / Schellenberg expectancy work

Large leaps tend to create reversal expectations; proximate continuation is a robust low-level expectation.

**Use for Skyforge:**

- a wide upward leap should usually be followed by a smaller descent;
- if we deny that continuation, do it for meaning, not by habit.

## 10. Reference-game-music implications

The existing game references remain useful, but the next pass should extract their **behavior**, not their surface style.

### *Rosalina in the Observatory*

Useful because it is overtly thematic and remains recognizable across progressively fuller orchestrations. The melody is a real object that can survive changes in arrangement.

**Implication:** melody before texture; phrase identity must be strong enough to persist under orchestration.

### *Space Junk Road*

Useful because the piano provides continuous thought/motion without requiring the lead melody to manufacture motion at every phrase boundary.

**Implication:** accompaniment can carry continuity while the melodic phrase is allowed to settle.

### *Outer Wilds*

Useful because musical events are deliberately placed and not allowed to become wallpaper.

**Implication:** a phrase does not need to remain perpetually unresolved to keep the listener engaged. Strategic absence and return can preserve meaning.

## 11. Concrete constraints for the next theme pass

These are now the default constraints unless the ear clearly argues otherwise.

### Register

- Establish a comfortable central tessitura.
- Place most phrase endings in the lower or middle half of that phrase's local range.
- A principal apex should normally occur **before** the phrase ending.
- Do not create a new registral high merely because a phrase boundary is approaching.

### Leap handling

- Upward leap >= perfect fourth: normally reverse afterward by step or third.
- Large leap near the end of a phrase requires explicit justification.
- Repeated "leap high and stop" gestures are prohibited.

### Cadence

- Open phrase: prefer harmonic openness over registral openness.
- Closed phrase: use stable scale degree, duration, meter, and harmony together.
- A low/mid ending on 2 or 5 over V is valid and often preferable to a high "question mark."

### Rhythm

- Phrase-final note or arrival should generally be longer than the immediately preceding active notes.
- Reduce onset density approaching a meaningful cadence.
- Avoid making every phrase boundary coincide mechanically with the bar line.

### Motive

- The opening face must remain singable.
- Motive recurrence should preserve rhythm/contour identity.
- Development should fragment or answer the idea, not merely transpose the entire phrase.

### Large-scale contour

- Do not impose a named contour category.
- For this role, favor an interior high region followed by enough descent to permit rhetorical closure.
- Reserve the highest point of the entire 16-bar theme for one structurally meaningful moment, not every phrase.

## 12. Acceptance gate for the next pass

The next theme is not accepted unless all of these are true:

1. the opening can be remembered after one or two hearings;
2. each phrase has a discernible apex separate from its endpoint;
3. at least two major phrase endings satisfy the ear by descending or returning toward the central tessitura;
4. open phrases remain open because of harmony/function, not simply because they end high;
5. large upward skips receive convincing reversals unless intentionally suspended;
6. the continuation feels necessary without literal shared-pitch chaining at every boundary;
7. the melody-only version still communicates phrase punctuation;
8. the whole line sounds thoughtful and wistful rather than tragic or merely ambient.

## Governing conclusion

The next pass should not optimize for "continuous connection."

It should optimize for **rhetorical inevitability**:

> rise when the thought intensifies, come down when the sentence wants to land, and let harmony or unfinished meaning—not habitual registral height—be what asks for the next sentence.
